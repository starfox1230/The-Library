Fluoro Field Guide

Understand the question before the sequence: what anatomy was changed, where a leak or obstruction could appear, which views make it visible, and what each image is meant to prove.

Training reference, not a live protocol. The attached GI Cliff Manual is dated 2023 and contains institution-specific directions. Confirm the current order, contrast choice, and sequence with your supervising radiologist and local protocol before every exam.
The mental model

Three questions organize the exam

01 · Why now?

What decision is this study supporting?

Leak or perforation, anastomotic integrity, transit/obstruction, tube position, stricture, or bladder/urethral injury each needs a different endpoint.

02 · What changed?

Rebuild the anatomy first

Read the operative note and recent CT. Name the operation, date, conduit/pouch/staple line or anastomosis, and any drains or catheters. Mark the target on the scout.

03 · What proves it?

Make every view answer something

Use position and contrast to coat or distend the target, then watch it in real time. Ask where contrast should go, where an abnormal track could exit, and what would be hidden in this projection.

04 · Close the loop

Check the endpoint

Document the key finding, save the required clips and spots, look for residual extraluminal contrast when indicated, and communicate an urgent suspected complication promptly.

How to think about altered anatomy

  1. Trace the path: Where does swallowed contrast enter, and where should it empty?
  2. Find the join or staple line: The anastomosis, staple line, repair, or wrap is the high-value target.
  3. Choose the position to reveal it: A dependent side, oblique, supine view, or upright view can uncover a small leak hidden on a single projection.
  4. Follow the contrast beyond the lumen: A leak is contrast outside the expected bowel contour; define its origin and extent, including any drain or collection when visible.
  5. Separate normal post-op shape from failure: A small pouch, rerouted limb, narrowed conduit, or wrap changes what “normal flow” looks like. Compare with the operation and expected anatomy.
Do not infer safety from one uneventful swallow. The manual’s study sequence is a local reference; the radiologist determines whether additional views, another contrast agent, or another modality is appropriate.

Quick map

Suspected post-op upper GI leak

Scout → water-soluble contrast first per manual → target the operative site in more than one position → consider barium only if the initial phase is reassuring and directed by the radiologist → post-scout.

Transit question

SBFT is a timed passage study. Start the clock at contrast administration; stop based on the requested endpoint and radiologist direction.

Tube question

Priority section · surgical anatomy

Post-operative upper GI leak study

The target is not “the whole stomach.” It is the repair, staple line, or anastomosis named in the operative history. A leak is extraluminal contrast; a negative study depends on adequately opacifying and inspecting the right segment.

Why do it? To look for contrast escaping from a recent upper-GI repair or altered anatomy, which can change immediate management. The study also shows whether contrast empties through the expected reconstructed route.

GI Cliff Manual sequence (2023 PDF, p. 16)

  1. 1
    Standing AP and LPO scout. Establish the baseline, orientation, surgical hardware, and expected region before contrast.
  2. 2
    Standing LPO, first Omnipaque swallow under live fluoroscopy. Watch bolus transit and the surgical site in real time for extravasation. The manual says to watch this swallow; it does not explicitly say to save a sequence.
  3. 3
    Standing LPO, second swallow: targeted 3-fps exposure series (digital acquisition). The manual specifies images at 3 frames/second at the target. In this guide, “acquisition series” means the stored, higher-quality frames at the surgical site, distinct from live fluoro; at 3 fps, frames are about 0.33 seconds apart. For hiatal-hernia repair, focus on lower esophagus/cardia; for sleeve and bypass, focus on the operative site. The manual does not specify the system’s menu label or whether the frames are stored as one run or as individual images.
  4. 4
    Opposite oblique. Repeat the two swallows. A second angle reduces overlap and may reveal a small leak.
  5. 5
    If the first phase looks reassuring, repeat with thick barium as directed. Manual specifies standing LPO and RPO swallows and spots focused on the surgical area.
  6. 6
    Horizontal table, LPO then RPO. Give thin barium through a straw and obtain targeted spots as directed.
  7. 7
    Post-scout after contrast clears the target. Look for residual extraluminal contrast; manual notes supine imaging or returning upright may be used when needed.
Terminology: Use live fluoroscopy for watching bolus transit in real time. In your institution’s wording, call this the targeted 3-fps exposure series (the higher-quality digital acquisition); use live fluoroscopy for real-time viewing. The manual itself specifies images at 3 frames/second but does not name the console mode or storage format. “Cine” can describe a saved sequence; “spot image” usually means one still.
There is a second, different sequence in your Notion transcript. The 2023 “Performing Leak Studies” note recalls standing AP obliques, water then Omnipaque, another set lying down, a barium phase while lying down, and a final standing assessment. It names E-POEM, G-POEM, and sleeve cases. It is explicitly a personal recording and says the final step was not done often. Keep it as a memory aid; ask the supervising radiologist which current local sequence applies.

Start with the operation: what anatomy should I expect?

Roux-en-Y gastric bypass

Two routes and a pouch

The manual’s schematic labels the gastrojejunostomy, Roux limb, and excluded gastric remnant (“stump”). Trace swallowed contrast from the pouch across the gastrojejunostomy into the Roux limb; target the staple/anastomotic region specified in the operative note.

Sleeve gastrectomy

A long staple line

The manual pairs a sleeve diagram with a contrast image marked by arrows along the staple line. Track the narrowed tubular stomach and inspect the full operated segment, not only the proximal stomach.

Fundoplication / hiatal repair

Wrap and lower esophagus

The manual’s paired image shows the contrast-filled distal esophagus entering a wrapped cardia, with the schematic overlay locating the fundoplication. For hiatal-hernia repair, the protocol directs attention to lower esophagus and cardia.

POEM / E-POEM / G-POEM

Use the procedure name

The transcript lists these as examples of post-op leak requests. Their target differs by procedure. The pages do not provide a POEM-specific step sequence; identify the treated segment with the radiologist instead of borrowing the sleeve/bypass target.

Contrast is a decision, not a memorized rule. The manual starts suspected post-op leak studies with low-osmolar water-soluble contrast; it allows barium only after the initial phase appears reassuring. Aspiration concern, fistula, obstruction, allergy history, and the operation may change the choice. The manual has specific contraindications and exceptions—consult it and the supervising radiologist.

Focused questions during the study

Upper GI

Esophagram: transit, motility, and post-op integrity

A routine esophagram asks how the esophagus distends, contracts, and empties. A leak protocol asks whether contrast escapes from a specific injured or repaired segment. They overlap in technique but are not interchangeable orders.

Purpose: Follow a bolus through the pharynx/esophagus and GE junction; assess transit and motility, or inspect a named postoperative repair when ordered.

What the manual says

  • For a water-soluble/postoperative leak study: video-record; standing scout AP, LPO, and right lateral; a water swallow is included to assess clinical aspiration; then low-osmolar water-soluble contrast in right lateral, AP, and LPO.
  • If the first phase looks reassuring, the manual describes barium swallows in LPO, AP, and right lateral. It specifies thick barium for total laryngectomy and Ivor-Lewis, thin barium for other indications.
  • For Ivor-Lewis, manual describes horizontal or 30° reverse Trendelenburg, LPO, thin barium via straw, then RPO spot. Finish with a post-scout after contrast clears the area; may acquire supine and return upright as needed.
  • For other single-contrast swallows, manual says sequence varies by indication and ability; thin barium, video and AP/LPO/RPO spots, observing emptying, motility, and reflux. Tablet use is as indicated and tolerated.
  • Significant esophageal stasis at study end may require removal with a red rubber catheter per manual and radiologist direction.
The manual’s “water swallow” is not a standalone aspiration clearance test. Use the ordered protocol and the radiologist’s judgment, especially with known/suspected aspiration or tracheoesophageal fistula.

Contrast choices: reason from risk and question

Possible free perforation or post-op leak

The local manual favors water-soluble contrast for the initial leak phase and says not to give oral barium if free GI perforation is suspected.

Suspected aspiration or TE fistula

The manual warns against oral iodinated water-soluble contrast in known/suspected aspiration or TE fistula. Do not improvise a substitute; confirm the agent and approach with the radiologist.

Obstruction or altered route

The manual says not to give oral barium with possible colonic obstruction. Clarify whether the order is a routine transit study or a water-soluble challenge.

Post-op anatomy

Operation, target, aspiration risk, and local agent availability govern. The manual’s product names and concentrations are local and may have changed.

See “DO NOTs in G.I. Radiology” and supplies reference in the source PDF before using this section in practice.

Single-contrast UGI versus routine UGI

The manual’s postoperative leak version lists 50–100 mL low-osmolar contrast followed by 5 oz thin/low-density barium if appropriate. The routine single-contrast UGI lists 5 oz thin/low-density barium. Its routine upper GI sequence uses air-contrast views to inspect stomach/duodenal anatomy, with positions chosen to profile body/antrum, lesser curve, fundus, bulb, and C-loop. The question determines which parts of the longer routine exam are needed.

Reason for the position changes: rotation redistributes contrast and gas so different walls and regions become visible; magnification is reserved for targeted anatomy. Do not copy the full routine UGI sequence into a focused post-op leak exam.
Transit study

Small bowel follow-through

This is a timed transit exam: how quickly oral/enteric contrast reaches the terminal ileum or colon, and whether an abnormal segment is seen along the way. Clarify the requested contrast path and endpoint before starting.

Barium pathway

Scout → timed radiographs → terminal ileum

  • Review AP abdomen scout.
  • Manual: 16 oz small-bowel barium within 5 minutes; the start time is when it is given.
  • At 15 minutes, prone PA abdomen image; send for radiologist review and timing instruction.
  • Usually 15 and 45 minutes, then every 30 minutes until contrast reaches terminal ileum.
  • When it reaches TI, supine fluoroscopy of the entire small bowel in two projections; spot TI and other pathology.
Water-soluble pathway

Scout → administered under fluoro → 4/8/12/24 h

  • Review AP abdomen scout.
  • Manual: 4 oz (120 cc) Gastrografin by NGT/PEG/oral route under fluoroscopy; clamp NGT/PEG.
  • Portable abdomen radiographs at 4, 8, 12, and 24 hours.
  • Terminate when contrast reaches colon or at 24 hours.
Your Notion quick note adds: check chart for NG tube and review CT for significant findings; it flags water-soluble as more common in its notes and records a separate attending preference for barium. Treat that as local workflow context, not a universal agent rule. The radiologist’s order and patient-specific risks control.
Reason through a delay: use the actual administration time, location of the leading contrast column, and requested endpoint. Ask whether a delayed film or fluoroscopy is needed; do not end solely because a preset interval elapsed.

Keep the clock honest

Device check

PEG / J-tube check

A tube check is a contrast study of the device course and its communication with bowel, not merely a tip-location image.

Question: Is the tube in the expected position and patent, and does injected contrast enter the expected lumen without extravasation?
  1. 1
    Supine setup and digital scout. Establish tube path and baseline anatomy.
  2. 2
    Radiologist injects contrast. Obtain digital images of the stomach supine and in right lateral position, per the manual.
  3. 3
    Inspect and finish. Radiologist assesses leak and placement; flush tube with water when finished.

Notion’s tube-check note also mentions AP and right lateral decubitus injections, saved videos, flush, and cap. The manual specifies digital scout and supine/right-lateral stomach views. Follow the current local version.

Tube reasoning checklist

Lower tract & bladder

RUG / pericatheter urethrogram

Retrograde contrast outlines the urethra to map a stricture or injury. Before positioning, ask whether there is pelvic trauma/fracture and whether a suprapubic catheter is present.

  • Manual: RPO with angle sponge; cover the pelvis and prepare towels. A barium tablet is used as a field marker in the manual.
  • If suprapubic catheter is present, the manual says the urethral study is still performed through the penis to show degree/site of stricture.
  • Radiologist holds the catheter and injects contrast while the technologist may fluoroscope and obtain images.
  • Known pelvic fracture: manual specifies white slide board with angle sponge. Trauma patients may need different catheter options available for the radiologist to select.
What to prove: the urethral course and caliber, the site/length of narrowing if present, and whether contrast escapes. Keep the whole relevant path in view; do not force positioning in an unstable or painful patient.

Cystogram and VCU: filling versus voiding

Cystogram

Fill the bladder, inspect the contour

Manual indication: possible bladder leak after prostatectomy, trauma, or other bladder surgery. Contrast is delivered through the existing Foley or suprapubic catheter; fill as much as tolerated or until full, then AP and both obliques. The manual describes draining afterward.

VCU

Add images during voiding

VCU adds an emptying phase after the bladder is filled. The manual describes AP and obliques, then catheter removal and voiding images under the local procedure. Male voiding studies are noted as uncommon in this manual; female VCU also adds voiding images.

Catheter handling and removal are clinician decisions. The manual contains site-specific catheter placement requirements and asks that the supervising clinician confirm whether voiding around a Foley is allowed. Do not disconnect, remove, or manipulate a catheter outside your role and local protocol.
Interpretive frame: inspect the bladder wall/contour and follow any contrast outside the expected bladder; during VCU, observe the outlet and urethral passage. The source pages are procedural and do not teach a complete injury classification—use the radiologist’s interpretation.

Lower GI study safety points from the manual

Your main Notion page has an “Enema” heading but no added steps. The attached manual does contain full-column and air-contrast enema procedures, so this guide keeps the points below at a caution level rather than reconstructing a complete procedure.

  • Manual says not to use a rectal balloon in known/suspected proctitis or rectal carcinoma; inflate only under fluoroscopy after rectal distention.
  • Manual says no barium enema with fulminant colitis/toxic megacolon, suspected free colonic perforation, or within 7 days after polypectomy; water-soluble agent is specified when free colonic perforation is suspected.
  • After J-pouch surgery, manual cautions against standard adult rectal tube and routine rectal exam; use current local instructions and the radiologist.
