These apparently separate areas may all represent components of a single PCA arterial distribution.\n9. The anterior and lateral temporal lobe is generally supplied by the MCA rather than the PCA.\n\n### H. Systematic vascular-territory reasoning\n1. Every suspected stroke should prompt the question: **What vascular distribution does this follow?**\n2. If a lesion fits a recognizable arterial distribution, an arterial infarct becomes much more likely.\n3. If it does not fit a single arterial distribution, considerations include:\n   - Multiple embolic infarcts.\n   - Watershed infarction from hypotension or hypoperfusion.\n   - Venous infarction.\n   - A noninfarct lesion.\n   - A metabolic or mitochondrial stroke-like lesion that does not respect arterial boundaries.\n4. The reader must recognize both when a lesion fits an arterial territory and when it does not.\n5. The purpose of learning detailed arterial anatomy is to direct visual attention toward expected sites of involvement.\n6. For a suspected PCA infarct, deliberately inspect:\n   - Occipital lobe.\n   - Medial and inferior temporal lobe.\n   - Hippocampus.\n   - Thalamus.\n   - Midbrain.\n   - Splenium.\n\n### I. PCA versus other posterior-circulation territories\n1. Supratentorial infarcts involving the occipital lobe, thalamus, medial temporal lobe, and hippocampus may all fit the PCA.\n2. A midbrain infarct may also fit proximal PCA branch involvement.\n3. A superior cerebellar infarct is in the superior cerebellar artery territory, not the PCA territory.\n4. Infarcts involving both PCA and superior cerebellar artery territories suggest more than one arterial distribution.\n5. Multiple noncontiguous posterior-circulation infarcts favor an embolic process rather than one simple territorial occlusion.\n6. A vertebral artery occlusion may serve as a potential source of emboli even when there is no persistent occlusion of every downstream artery.\n7. Typical watershed zones include:\n   - MCA–PCA border zones.\n   - ACA–MCA border zones.\n8. A complete PCA-territory infarct is not the same as a PCA watershed infarct.\n\n## II. Occipital, temporal, and parietal lobe localization\n\n### A. Value of sagittal imaging\n1. Sagittal images are often the most useful for determining which posterior cerebral lobes are involved.\n2. The hippocampal head and body can be followed sagittally to establish temporal-lobe involvement.\n3. If an abnormality clearly extends through the hippocampus or medial temporal tissue, it is not confined to the occipital lobe.\n\n### B. Parietal–occipital boundary\n1. The parieto-occipital sulcus provides a true anatomic landmark between the parietal and occipital lobes.\n2. It is most easily identified on sagittal imaging.\n\n### C. Temporal–occipital boundary\n1. There is no comparably distinct sulcus that cleanly separates the temporal and occipital lobes on their lateral/inferior surfaces.\n2. The boundary is partly a conventional or imaginary line.\n3. The temporal lobe extends farther posteriorly than is often intuitively expected.\n4. The occipital lobe is relatively small.\n5. Distinguishing posterior temporal from occipital involvement can therefore be difficult and sometimes cannot be done with complete confidence.\n\n### D. Reporting implication\n1. A posterior lesion involving both the occipital lobe and posterior/medial temporal lobe can be called **occipitotemporal**.\n2. “Occipitotemporal” may be more accurate than “parieto-occipital” when there is no convincing parietal involvement.\n3. Calling such a lesion simply “occipital” may be acceptable but less anatomically complete.\n\n## III. Hemorrhage associated with a PCA-territory infarct\n\n### A. Initial question when an intraparenchymal hemorrhage is identified\n1. Do not stop at identifying the hemorrhage.\n2. Ask what caused it.\n3. Potential causes include:\n   - Hypertensive hemorrhage.\n   - Cerebral amyloid angiopathy.\n   - Trauma.\n   - Hemorrhagic metastasis or another underlying lesion.\n   - Hemorrhagic conversion of an ischemic infarct.\n4. Use the same systematic approach applied to suspected ischemia:\n   - Determine the distribution.\n   - Decide whether it conforms to an arterial territory.\n   - Consider venous or nonvascular causes when it does not.\n\n### B. Diffusion abnormality surrounding a hematoma\n1. Diffusion abnormality around a hematoma does not automatically prove a separate ischemic infarct.\n2. A very large hematoma with substantial mass effect can:\n   - Stretch or compress adjacent white-matter fibers.\n   - Compromise local perfusion.\n   - Produce peripheral tissue infarction.\n3. This mechanism is more plausible with a large hemorrhage producing significant mass effect or midline shift.\n4. A relatively small hematoma is unlikely to create a broad arterial-territory pattern merely through mechanical stretching.\n5. If diffusion restriction and hemorrhage together conform to a PCA territory, hemorrhagic conversion of a PCA infarct should be strongly considered.\n\n### C. Recognizing petechial hemorrhage\n1. Petechial hemorrhage is not the same morphologic process as an organized parenchymal hematoma.\n2. On susceptibility-sensitive imaging, petechial hemorrhage may appear:\n   - Gyriform.\n   - Punctate.\n   - Curvilinear.\n   - Brush-like along the cortex.\n3. A useful visual analogy is black paint lightly brushed along the cortical surface.\n4. Petechial hemorrhage may be poorly visible on CT.\n5. CT may show only subtle linear or “squiggly” areas of hyperdensity.\n6. Petechial hemorrhage often follows the involved infarcted cortex.\n7. In a PCA infarct, petechial hemorrhage may extend through the occipital and medial temporal portions of the infarct.\n\n### D. Recognizing a parenchymal hematoma\n1. A parenchymal hematoma is a more organized, space-occupying collection of blood.\n2. It appears as a focal mass of blood rather than merely gyriform susceptibility.\n3. It may:\n   - Produce its own mass effect.\n   - Generate surrounding vasogenic edema.\n   - Have a clearer CT correlate.\n4. Organized focal blood should be distinguished from additional petechial susceptibility surrounding it.\n5. In difficult cases, CT can help identify which portion represents an actual hematoma.\n\n### E. Measuring a hematoma on MRI\n1. T2-weighted imaging may be the easiest sequence on which to measure an acute or early-subacute hematoma.\n2. Follow the organized dark component of the blood products on T2.\n3. Do not measure the entire region of surrounding petechial susceptibility as though it were all one hematoma.\n4. Measure the portion that appears organized and mass-like.\n5. The hematoma may extend obliquely across multiple slices, such as toward the tentorium.\n6. Its entire extent may not be captured on one image.\n7. Follow it through adjacent slices and use the CT correlate when available.\n8. Exact measurement was considered less important than correctly distinguishing:\n   - Organized hematoma.\n   - Petechial hemorrhage.\n   - Hemorrhage extending outside the infarct.\n\n### F. Hemorrhagic PCA infarct pattern\n1. A combination of the following supports hemorrhagic conversion:\n   - Diffusion restriction in a PCA distribution.\n   - Occipital and medial temporal involvement.\n   - Petechial hemorrhage within that territory.\n   - A focal parenchymal hematoma within the infarct.\n2. The anterior hippocampal head may be spared because portions of its blood supply may arise from the anterior choroidal rather than the PCA circulation.\n3. Even without thalamic or midbrain involvement, occipital and medial temporal involvement can still represent a PCA infarct.\n4. Thalamic and midbrain structures should nevertheless be deliberately inspected.\n\n## IV. Diffusion restriction, DWI, ADC, and T2 blackout\n\n### A. Fundamental definition\n1. True diffusion restriction is determined primarily by the **ADC map**.\n2. Dark signal on ADC indicates restricted diffusion.\n3. The definition of restriction does not require the DWI image itself to be bright.\n\n### B. Typical acute infarct\n1. Usually appears:\n   - Bright on DWI.\n   - Dark on ADC.\n2. This is the familiar appearance of restricted diffusion.\n\n### C. T2 shine-through\n1. T2 shine-through generally appears:\n   - Bright on DWI.\n   - Not correspondingly dark on ADC; the ADC may also remain bright.\n2. The DWI brightness is being driven by the underlying T2 signal rather than genuine restriction.\n\n### D. T2 blackout\n1. T2 blackout can appear:\n   - Dark or relatively unimpressive on DWI.\n   - Dark on ADC.\n2. It still represents genuine diffusion restriction because the ADC is reduced.\n3. The DWI fails to look bright because the underlying lesion is markedly T2 hypointense.\n4. T2 blackout is conceptually the inverse of T2 shine-through:\n   - Shine-through: bright DWI and bright/nonreduced ADC.\n   - Blackout: dark DWI and dark ADC.\n5. Acute or early-subacute blood products can produce T2 blackout.\n6. Lymphoma is another classic setting:\n   - The lesion may be T2 intermediate or dark.\n   - DWI may not look strikingly bright.\n   - ADC may still be clearly dark, confirming restriction.\n\n### E. Ambiguity of saying “associated diffusion restriction”\n1. The phrase can be unclear when a hematoma is present.\n2. It may refer merely to restricted diffusion within blood products.\n3. It may instead refer to adjacent ischemic brain tissue.\n4. A more useful report specifies:\n   - Whether restriction is confined to the hematoma.\n   - Whether it extends through an arterial territory.\n   - Whether there is petechial hemorrhage.\n   - Whether there is a parenchymal hematoma.\n\n## V. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy as a differential consideration\n\n1. A lobar parenchymal hematoma without an associated territorial infarct raises consideration of cerebral amyloid angiopathy.\n2. A patient in the mid-50s is relatively young for typical amyloid angiopathy, although it may still enter the differential.\n3. SWI should be examined for additional microhemorrhages.\n4. A pattern supporting amyloid angiopathy would include:\n   - Multiple lobar microhemorrhages.\n   - Relative sparing of the deep gray nuclei and other deep structures.\n5. Absence of this pattern weakens, but does not by itself completely exclude, amyloid angiopathy.\n6. In the discussed case, a PCA-territory infarct with petechial hemorrhage and parenchymal hematoma was considered more convincing than a bland amyloid-related lobar hemorrhage.\n\n## VI. Hemorrhagic transformation terminology and Heidelberg classification\n\n### A. Two principal morphologic categories\n1. **Hemorrhagic infarction/petechial hemorrhage**\n2. **Parenchymal hematoma**\n3. Both are technically forms of hemorrhagic conversion.\n4. “Hemorrhagic conversion” alone is therefore insufficiently specific.\n\n### B. Hemorrhagic infarction\n1. Heidelberg terminology uses **HI** for hemorrhagic infarction.\n2. HI corresponds to petechial hemorrhage.\n3. **HI1**\n   - Scattered small petechiae.\n4. **HI2**\n   - More confluent petechiae.\n5. Typical appearance:\n   - Gyriform or brush-like susceptibility.\n   - Punctate cortical blood products.\n   - No organized, space-occupying hematoma.\n\n### C. Parenchymal hematoma\n1. Heidelberg terminology uses **PH** for parenchymal hematoma.\n2. PH is an organized collection of blood within the infarct.\n3. The discussed focal hematoma, considered by itself, was described as approximately **class 1C**:\n   - Parenchymal hematoma.\n   - Occupying less than approximately 30% of the infarcted tissue.\n4. Estimating the percentage requires comparing:\n   - The volume of organized hematoma.\n   - The total volume of diffusion-abnormal infarct.\n5. Whether the estimate is 25% or close to 30% is less important than recognizing whether the hematoma occupies the majority of the infarct.\n\n### D. Hemorrhage outside the infarct\n1. A major additional branch point is whether hemorrhage extends beyond the infarcted tissue.\n2. Concerning sites include:\n   - Intraventricular space.\n   - Subarachnoid space.\n   - Subdural space.\n   - Other extra-axial compartments.\n3. Extension outside the infarct moves the event into a class 3 category in the classification discussed.\n4. The case was described as:\n   - Locally resembling class 1C based on the intraparenchymal hematoma.\n   - Overall class 3B because of intraventricular extension.\n\n### E. Clinical and reporting importance\n1. The most important practical distinction is:\n   - Petechial hemorrhage only.\n   - Parenchymal hematoma.\n2. Petechial hemorrhage confined to the infarct often does not alter treatment in the same way as a true parenchymal hematoma.\n3. A parenchymal hematoma may cause anticoagulation or antithrombotic treatment to be withheld.\n4. The practical treatment distinction was described as occurring approximately at the transition from petechial hemorrhage to parenchymal hematoma—the 1B/1C boundary in the terminology being discussed.\n5. Reports should therefore avoid using only the nonspecific phrase “hemorrhagic conversion.”\n6. More useful formulations include:\n   - “Acute infarct with petechial hemorrhage and no parenchymal hematoma.”\n   - “Acute infarct with parenchymal hematoma.”\n   - “Acute infarct with petechial hemorrhage, parenchymal hematoma, and intraventricular extension.”\n7. It is not always necessary to include the formal Heidelberg class in the report.\n8. Correctly describing the morphology is more important than naming the category.\n\n## VII. Chronic infarction, cortical laminar necrosis, and enhancement\n\n### A. Determining infarct age\n1. Evaluate:\n   - DWI.\n   - ADC.\n   - Precontrast T1.\n   - Postcontrast T1.\n   - Morphologic evidence of chronic infarction.\n2. A chronic infarct may contain superimposed acute or subacute ischemia.\n3. When timing remains uncertain, “acute/subacute on chronic infarction” is a reasonable formulation.\n\n### B. Cortical laminar necrosis\n1. Cortical laminar necrosis may be intrinsically bright on precontrast T1.\n2. This intrinsic T1 hyperintensity can be mistaken for cortical or gyriform enhancement.\n3. True enhancement requires comparison of equivalent precontrast and postcontrast images.\n4. Comparing a sagittal precontrast series with an axial postcontrast series is suboptimal.\n5. Apparent postcontrast cortical brightness may simply represent pre-existing T1 hyperintensity from cortical laminar necrosis.\n\n### C. Benefit of three-dimensional sequences\n1. Three-dimensional T1 and FLAIR acquisitions allow axial, coronal, and sagittal reformats.\n2. They facilitate direct plane-matched precontrast and postcontrast comparison.\n3. They reduce the problem of trying to compare sequences obtained in different planes.\n\n### D. Reporting construction from the discussed case\n1. The report should explicitly identify:\n   - The PCA territory.\n   - Occipitotemporal involvement.\n   - Petechial hemorrhage.\n   - Parenchymal hematoma.\n   - Intraventricular extension.\n2. “IPH” can be ambiguous when both petechial hemorrhage and a true hematoma are present.\n3. Directly describing each hemorrhagic component is more informative.\n\n## VIII. Splenium of the corpus callosum: PCA infarct versus CLOCC\n\n### A. Splenial arterial supply\n1. The splenium of the corpus callosum is supplied by the PCA.\n2. The responsible supply is usually from a distal P3 branch.\n3. It may arise from the parieto-occipital branch.\n4. This is anatomically plausible because the parieto-occipital branch lies closer to the splenium than the calcarine branch.\n\n### B. Interpreting splenial DWI signal\n1. Mild DWI hyperintensity should not automatically be called an infarct.\n2. The ADC map must be evaluated.\n3. If the ADC abnormality is unimpressive, the finding may not warrant strong emphasis.\n\n### C. Expected PCA infarct morphology\n1. A unilateral PCA infarct involving the splenium is generally:\n   - Eccentric or off-midline.\n   - More irregular in shape.\n   - Predominantly unilateral.\n2. It may reach the midline.\n3. It may extend slightly across the midline because arterial territories overlap.\n4. It should not extend broadly into the contralateral splenium as though centered symmetrically.\n\n### D. Expected CLOCC morphology\n1. A typical cytotoxic lesion of the corpus callosum, or **CLOCC**, is:\n   - Centered directly at the midline.\n   - Relatively small.\n   - Round or oval.\n   - More symmetric than a PCA infarct.\n2. Eccentric irregular splenial involvement favors infarction.\n3. A small round central lesion favors CLOCC.\n\n## IX. Cervical spine trauma: degenerative change versus occult injury\n\n### A. Initial problem\n1. Focal marrow or endplate edema after trauma may represent:\n   - Degenerative change.\n   - Bone contusion.\n   - Tiny occult nondisplaced fracture.\n   - Fracture through a pre-existing osteophyte.\n2. The distinction can remain uncertain even after MRI.\n\n### B. Use of prior CT examinations\n1. Review the current CT.\n2. Review older CT examinations whenever available.\n3. A fragmented osteophyte that was present previously is not an acute fracture.\n4. Stability across prior studies is strong evidence of chronic degenerative change.\n5. Even a fragment that appears indeterminate on one CT can be classified as nonacute when it is unchanged from prior imaging.\n\n### C. MRI findings that raise suspicion\n1. New focal marrow edema.\n2. Trace prevertebral edema or fluid.\n3. A possible prevertebral hematoma.\n4. Soft-tissue edema in the setting of trauma should prompt inspection for:\n   - Ligamentous injury.\n   - Osteophyte fracture.\n   - Occult vertebral fracture.\n   - Cord injury.\n\n### D. Features of occult fracture\n1. MRI can reveal fractures that are occult on CT.\n2. Typical occult compression fractures often demonstrate:\n   - A linear or somewhat irregular STIR-hyperintense fracture line.\n   - Signal extending through a substantial portion of the endplate or vertebral body.\n3. A tiny highly focal area of edema without a convincing fracture line is less specific.\n4. In such an edge case, it may be inappropriate to state confidently that an acute fracture is present.\n\n### E. Features favoring degenerative endplate change\n1. Bright signal on STIR confirms edema but does not establish its cause.\n2. A matching or similar abnormality on the opposite side of the disc space supports degeneration.\n3. Degenerative changes can be at different stages on the two sides.\n4. More fatty or Modic type 2-like change may be:\n   - Bright on T1.\n   - Bright on T2.\n   - Less conspicuous on STIR.\n5. A more edematous degenerative focus may be brighter on STIR and less conspicuous on T1.\n6. The coexistence of these different signal patterns may favor degenerative change over trauma.\n\n### F. Appropriate hedging\n1. When imaging is equivocal, a reasonable differential is:\n   - Degenerative endplate marrow edema.\n   - Tiny occult nondisplaced fracture.\n   - Bone contusion.\n2. Do not overstate the likelihood of fracture if the morphology is not convincing.\n3. Trace prevertebral soft-tissue abnormality can be described without proving that it represents hemorrhage.\n\n### G. Prevertebral edema, fluid, and hematoma terminol
