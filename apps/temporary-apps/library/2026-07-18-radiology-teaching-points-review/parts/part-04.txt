Limitations of combined whole-spine studies\n1. Combined cervical, thoracic, and lumbar MRI examinations often sacrifice detail.\n2. The large field of view makes evaluation of:\n   - Subtle fractures.\n   - Cord signal.\n   - Foraminal stenosis.\n   - Fine ligamentous abnormalities\n   more difficult.\n3. Splitting interpretation among different readers can also be awkward when one acquisition contains portions of multiple spinal regions.\n4. When all regions are acquired together, one approach is to interpret the entire combined examination rather than disregard sequences assigned to another region.\n\n## X. Traumatic cord contusion without fracture\n\n### A. Mechanism\n1. Patients with pre-existing degenerative canal stenosis may sustain spinal cord injury without:\n   - A vertebral fracture.\n   - A major ligament tear.\n2. Hyperflexion or hyperextension can transiently compress the cord within an already stenotic canal.\n3. This can produce a cord contusion or edema despite no definite osseous injury.\n\n### B. Example teaching case\n1. A trauma patient initially considered for myelography instead underwent MRI.\n2. MRI was preferable because it directly demonstrated a clear cord signal abnormality.\n3. The case showed:\n   - Pre-existing degenerative canal stenosis.\n   - Trace prevertebral fluid.\n   - No definite fracture.\n   - Convincing cord signal abnormality.\n4. This represented the type of injury that could be missed if evaluation were limited to osseous findings.\n\n### C. Clinical correlation by level\n1. A cervical cord lesion should be correlated with upper-extremity as well as lower-extremity symptoms.\n2. Neural elements at approximately C4–C5 and below contribute to pathways supplying the arms and ultimately the brachial plexus.\n3. A cervical cord lesion affects central/upper-motor-neuron pathways, not the brachial plexus itself.\n4. A lesion spanning the mid-to-lower cervical cord would generally be expected to produce some arm findings if clinically significant.\n5. Isolated leg numbness is less specific for an acute cervical cord injury.\n6. Isolated lower-extremity symptoms may fit a lumbar canal or cauda equina process better.\n\n### D. Threshold for calling cord signal abnormality\n1. Mild heterogeneity in the cord can be artifactual.\n2. Some degree of apparent signal variation can reasonably be dismissed when:\n   - It is only seen on a low-quality sagittal sequence.\n   - It is not reproduced on axial images.\n   - The field of view is large.\n   - The clinical history does not support cervical myelopathy.\n3. The threshold should be lower for calling true injury when:\n   - The abnormality is convincing on both sagittal and axial images.\n   - There are acute upper-extremity symptoms.\n   - There is central cord syndrome.\n   - There is acute quadriparesis or quadriplegia.\n   - The signal occurs at a severely stenotic level after trauma.\n4. Clinical history can appropriately shift the interpretation in either direction.\n\n### E. Application to the discussed equivocal case\n1. Sagittal T2/STIR suggested possible cord signal abnormality.\n2. The finding was less convincing on axial images.\n3. The whole-spine technique and large field of view increased the likelihood of artifact.\n4. Chart review showed:\n   - Leg numbness had been occurring for approximately two years.\n   - The patient fell after the legs became numb while standing.\n   - There was loss of consciousness.\n5. This history reduced concern that the questionable cervical signal represented an acute traumatic cord injury.\n6. Moderate-to-severe lumbar canal stenosis at L2–L3 and L3–L4 could better explain isolated lower-extremity symptoms.\n7. In that context, the questionable cervical cord signal could reasonably be treated as potentially artifactual.\n\n### F. Recommended reporting approach\n1. State that the apparent cord signal is present on sagittal T2/STIR images.\n2. State that it is less convincing on axial images.\n3. Note that whole-spine technique and large field of view limit evaluation.\n4. Correlate with the absence or presence of upper-extremity symptoms.\n5. Recommend a dedicated cervical spine MRI if there is clinical concern for cervical myelopathy.\n6. A representative formulation:\n   - “Questionable cervical cord signal abnormality on wide-field-of-view sagittal images, less convincing on axial images and potentially artifactual due to whole-spine technique. If there is clinical concern for cervical myelopathy or upper-extremity involvement, consider dedicated cervical spine MRI.”\n7. In a patient with a classic acute central cord presentation, the interpretation should come down more firmly in favor of cord edema or contusion.\n\n## XI. Efficient cervical spine MRI interpretation workflow\n\n### A. Basic hanging protocol\n1. Display sagittal T2 and axial T2 simultaneously.\n2. Keep the current examination and relevant prior examination visible together.\n3. For trauma cases, also keep the CT open.\n4. Zoom sufficiently so that the spinal canal, cord, and foramina are not excessively small.\n\n### B. Synchronizing scroll direction\n1. CT and MRI stacks may initially scroll in opposite anatomic directions.\n2. Reverse one stack so all series scroll in the same direction.\n3. One method is to change the CT stack to descending image-number order.\n4. After adjustment:\n   - Scrolling upward moves superiorly on every series.\n   - Scrolling toward the reader moves in the same direction on every series.\n5. Matching scroll direction makes level-by-level comparison faster and less error-prone.\n\n### C. Use both two-dimensional and three-dimensional axial T2 sequences\n1.
