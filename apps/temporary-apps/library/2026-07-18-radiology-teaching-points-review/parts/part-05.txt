With priors, an effective layout can include:\n   - Current 2D axial.\n   - Current 3D axial/reformats.\n   - Prior 2D axial.\n   - Prior 3D axial/reformats.\n   - Current and prior sagittal images.\n\n### D. Level-by-level evaluation\n1. Move systematically from one level to the next.\n2. At each level, evaluate:\n   - Disc morphology.\n   - Endplate osteophytes.\n   - Uncovertebral hypertrophy.\n   - Facet disease.\n   - Ligamentum flavum thickening.\n   - Canal stenosis.\n   - Foraminal stenosis.\n   - Cord deformation.\n   - Cord signal.\n3. Find the image at which each neural foramen is maximally open.\n4. Grade the foramen on the image that best shows its true caliber rather than on an oblique slice that merely makes it look narrow.\n\n### E. Primary planes for stenosis\n1. Cervical **foraminal stenosis** is graded primarily on axial T2 imaging.\n2. Cervical **canal stenosis** is graded primarily on sagittal T2 imaging.\n3. These are primary planes, not exclusive planes.\n4. Cross-reference:\n   - Axial T2.\n   - Sagittal T2.\n   - 3D reformats.\n   - CT.\n5. Axial images may be slightly off-axis through the foramen and overstate stenosis.\n6. Sagittal images or CT may show that the foramen is actually more open.\n7. Conversely, CT may reveal an uncovertebral spur and calcified disc that make stenosis more significant than it first appears on MRI.\n\n## XII. Using CT to characterize degenerative cervical disease\n\n### A. Disc bulge versus disc-osteophyte complex\n1. Do not automatically call every low-signal ventral abnormality a disc-osteophyte complex.\n2. Use **disc-osteophyte complex** when:\n   - Both disc material and an endplate osteophyte are present.\n   - Or only MRI is available and the components cannot be confidently distinguished.\n3. If CT demonstrates no endplate osteophyte, the lesion should be called a disc bulge rather than a disc-osteophyte complex.\n4. A disc may be partially or peripherally calcified without constituting an endplate osteophyte.\n5. A correct description in that setting is **partially calcified disc bulge**.\n6. In the discussed case, most levels demonstrated disc bulges.\n7. One level had a genuine endplate osteophyte component and could appropriately be called a disc-osteophyte complex.\n\n### B. Clinical importance of the distinction\n1. Disc material and fixed endplate osteophytes are not surgically equivalent.\n2. A simple discectomy may not fully relieve stenosis caused by substantial fixed osteophytes unless the osteophytes are also addressed.\n3. Correctly identifying the osseous component helps the surgeon understand the mechanism of compression.\n\n### C. OPLL and the posterior longitudinal ligament\n1. CT is particularly useful for diagnosing ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament.\n2. Thick or low-signal material behind the vertebral bodies on MRI can mimic OPLL.\n3. If CT shows no ossification, OPLL should not be diagnosed.\n4. Merely describing nonspecific posterior longitudinal ligament thickening is often not clinically useful.\n5. The more important distinctions are:\n   - True OPLL.\n   - Disc extrusion.\n   - Other compressive disc or osseous pathology.\n6. The posterior longitudinal ligament itself is not ordinarily a structure that would be resected merely because it appears thickened.\n\n### D. Ligamentum flavum\n1. Marked ligamentum flavum thickening can make a significant contribution to canal stenosis.\n2. It should be assessed along with the ventral disc or osteophyte component.\n3. Canal narrowing may therefore result from combined ventral and dorsal compression.\n\n## XIII. Terminology for stenosis\n\n### A. Concise preferred wording\n1. **Canal stenosis** is sufficient.\n2. “Spinal canal stenosis” is understandable but adds an unnecessary word.\n3. **Foraminal stenosis** is sufficient.\n4. “Neural foraminal stenosis” is understandable but generally redundant.\n5. Standard classification systems use the concise terms:\n   - Canal stenosis.\n   - Foraminal stenosis.\n\n### B. Cervical foraminal stenosis\n1. The Kim system was cited as the preferred system for cervical foraminal stenosis.\n2. In the version described, there is no formal “mild” category; the principal categories progress from no significant stenosis to moderate and severe.\n3. Radiologists commonly use “mild” in practice despite this.\n4. Very mild narrowing may be functionally insignificant and may not need to be emphasized.\n5. The essential task is identifying clinically meaningful moderate or severe narrowing.\n\n### C. Lumbar terminology\n1. The Lee systems include separate approaches for:\n   - Lumbar central canal stenosis.\n   - Lumbar foraminal stenosis.\n2. The formal term “lumbar central canal stenosis” may appear in the literature.\n3. In routine reporting, “lumbar canal stenosis” is usually sufficiently clear.\n4. Similarly, “lumbar foraminal stenosis” is sufficient without adding “neural.”\n\n## XIV. Grading cervical foraminal stenosis\n\n### A. General method\n1. Evaluate the axial image on which the foramen is maximally open.\n2. Compare with:\n   - The opposite side.\n   - Adjacent levels.\n   - Sagittal images.\n   - CT or CT reformats.\n3. A tiny residual sliver of foraminal opening suggests substantial stenosis.\n4. An uncovertebral spur plus a calcified disc bulge may compress the exiting nerve more than either component alone.\n\n### B. Moderate versus severe\n1. Grading has some unavoidable subjectivity.\n2. Moderate-to-severe may be appropriate when the foramen looks very tight but definite nerve deformation is not unequivocal.\n3. Severe should be reserved for convincing compression or deformation of the exiting nerve.\n4. The nerve should look truly “smashed” or morphologically altered rather than merely surrounded by less fat.\n5. If another plane shows that the foramen is actually open, downgrade the apparent axial stenosis.\n6. Do not call severe stenosis based on an isolated poor-quality or off-axis image.\n7. At C7–T1, apparent narrowing on one axial view may prove mild when sagittal images or CT reformats show a more open foramen.\n\n### C. Normal comparison\n1.
